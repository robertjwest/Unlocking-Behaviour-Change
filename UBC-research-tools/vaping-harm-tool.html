When do changes in vaping mean less harm?

Set your own judgements of the harms of smoking, vaping and dual use. The model estimates the change in population harm when vaping rises or falls: how much of a rise must come from smokers switching, or how few of those who stop vaping can turn to smoking, for there to be a net public health benefit.

What the evidence suggests about switchers

The middle view assumes 20% of new vapers are smokers who would otherwise still smoke. Evidence from England points to roughly 15–30%:

  • After the 2025 disposable vape ban, adult vaping was 5.2 points below the no-ban counterfactual by February 2026, while smoking was only 0.9 points higher (−0.5 to +2.2): a ratio of about 0.17 for marginal vapers (Smoking Toolkit Study, preprint).
  • Time-series analyses estimate that e-cigarettes have helped about 30,000–50,000 additional smokers a year to quit since they became popular (2007–2022 analysis), against about 2.2 million ex-smokers who vape (Jackson et al. 2024). That suggests perhaps 20–30% of ex-smoker vapers would otherwise still smoke, before allowing for relapse, deaths and later quitting.
  • About one in ten ex-smokers who vape quit before 2011, when e-cigarettes became popular, so they belong in the “would have quit anyway” group.
  • None of these captures relapse prevention, which would push the true share up.

Where do people who stop vaping go? Shares are rescaled to total 100%.

What the evidence suggests about falls
  • After the 2025 disposable vape ban, adult vaping was 5.2 points below the no-ban counterfactual by February 2026, while smoking was 0.9 points higher (−0.5 to +2.2): about 17% of the fall went to smoking. Among young adults smoking was possibly 3.3 points higher (−0.5 to +7.1) (Smoking Toolkit Study, preprint).
  • This is short-term evidence: later relapse to smoking among ex-smokers who stop vaping would push the share up.
  • People lost in a fall are not the mirror image of those gained in a rise: after a ban they are often younger, recent or occasional users.

How the model works

  • Harm per user = weighted average of the four harm scores, so a smoker scores 100.
  • Population harm = adults × prevalence × harm per user, expressed as the number of exclusive smokers carrying the same total harm (smoking-equivalents).
  • Complete switchers move from smoking to vaping; new dual users add vaping to smoking; would-have-quit vapers and never-smokers add vaping harm, and some never-smokers also take up smoking.
  • In the simplest case, a 1-point rise in vaping breaks even if smoking falls by at least the vaping/smoking harm ratio; a 1-point fall breaks even if smoking rises by no more than that ratio.
  • In a fall, people who stop vaping either stop nicotine, smoke instead (relapse, never switching, or starting), keep smoking if they were dual users, or switch to another nicotine product.

Main limitations

  • A steady-state comparison, not a forecast: deaths follow decades of use, and quitting and relapse are not modelled.
  • Scores are judgements: the tool makes them explicit rather than settling them.
  • Ignores harm to others, intensity of use and differences between products.
  • Prevalence defaults are rounded; check smokinginengland.info for the latest figures.
